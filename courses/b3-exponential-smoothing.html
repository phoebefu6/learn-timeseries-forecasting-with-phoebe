Part 1 · covers FPP3 ch.8

Recent past matters more 6 min live

A moving average treats the last 7 days as equally important and everything older as worthless. Exponential smoothing is smarter: every past point counts, but its weight decays geometrically as it recedes. One parameter, alpha, sets how fast the memory fades - high alpha reacts quickly, low alpha is steady.

LiveThe three members of the ETS family3 min▶

You add one component at a time:

  • Simple exponential smoothing (SES) - tracks the level only. Good for a flat, trendless, seasonless series. One parameter: alpha.
  • Holt's linear trend - adds a smoothed trend term (beta). Now the forecast can slope up or down instead of going flat.
  • Holt-Winters - adds a smoothed seasonal term (gamma). This is the one that captures Lumen's weekly rhythm - and the one that wins on this data.
Real world

ETS is the workhorse. Exponential smoothing is often the quiet default in retail and operations forecasting: it needs little data, trains in milliseconds, and is hard to beat on short horizons. Many teams ship Holt-Winters and only reach for ARIMA or ML when a backtest proves it earns its keep.

Part 2 · covers FPP3 ch.8

Level, trend, and season, each updated 5 min live

Holt-Winters keeps three running estimates and nudges each one every day: the level (where we are), the trend (how fast we are moving), and the seasonal offsets (the repeating weekly shape). The forecast is simply level + horizon x trend + the right seasonal offset.

statsmodels' ExponentialSmoothing covers the whole family - you just switch trend and seasonal on:

On the Lumen series the ranking is unambiguous: Holt-Winters, which alone captures the weekly season, posts the lowest error.

Forecast Lumen with a prediction interval ★ 8 min · build your own

Fit Holt-Winters and report the interval, not just the point.

Fit ExponentialSmoothing with additive trend and 7-day seasonality on the train set.

Forecast the horizon and pull a prediction interval from the model's residual spread.

Score MAPE on the holdout and compare it to the seasonal-naive baseline from b1.

★ Run it now (Python) - Holt-Winters + 80% intervalimport numpy as np from statsmodels.tsa.holtwinters import ExponentialSmoothing hw = ExponentialSmoothing(train, trend="add", seasonal="add", seasonal_periods=7).fit() fc = hw.forecast(len(test)) # an 80% interval from the in-sample residual spread, widening with horizon resid = train - hw.fittedvalues sigma = resid.std() z = 1.28 steps = np.arange(1, len(test) + 1) lo = fc - z * sigma * np.sqrt(steps) hi = fc + z * sigma * np.sqrt(steps) mape = (np.abs(test.values - fc.values) / test.values).mean() * 100 print(f"Holt-Winters MAPE: {mape:.2f}% (beat seasonal-naive from b1?)") print("interval widens with horizon:", round(hi.iloc[0]-lo.iloc[0]), "->", round(hi.iloc[-1]-lo.iloc[-1]))
Real world

Simple, but not naive. Exponential smoothing has a formal statistical form (the ETS state-space models in FPP3) that produces principled prediction intervals and can auto-select the best trend/season combination. That is why it remains a first-line production method - you get calibrated uncertainty almost for free.

Check yourself

Three questions before you go 🎯 ◐ 90 seconds

1 · What does the alpha parameter in exponential smoothing control?

Alpha sets the decay of the weights. High alpha weights the recent past heavily (twitchy); low alpha spreads weight further back (steady).

2 · Which ETS member captures a weekly pattern?

SES tracks level only; Holt adds trend; Holt-Winters adds the seasonal term (gamma) that models the repeating weekly offsets.

3 · Why report a prediction interval with the forecast?

Forecasts are uncertain. The interval communicates that honestly, and it should widen further out because uncertainty compounds with the horizon.
