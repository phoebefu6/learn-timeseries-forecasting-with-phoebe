Part 1 · covers FPP3 foundations

A series is four things stacked together 6 min live

Strip away the jargon and any time series is a sum of four parts: a trend (the slow drift up or down), seasonality (a pattern that repeats on a fixed period - here, a weekly rhythm), a longer cycle, and noise (the irregular wiggle). Forecasting is the craft of pulling those apart, projecting the ones that persist, and being honest about the one that does not.

LiveFrequency, index, and the gaps that break everything3 min▶

Before any model, the series has to be clean and regular:

  • A real datetime index - not strings. pandas needs to know Tuesday follows Monday, so it can resample, shift, and roll.
  • One fixed frequency - daily, weekly, hourly. Raw logs are irregular; you resample to a grid first.
  • No silent gaps - a missing day is not a zero. Decide explicitly: fill, interpolate, or flag. A gap treated as zero will wreck seasonality.
Real world

Frequency is a modelling choice, not a given. Lumen's raw analytics arrive per-session with timestamps. Forecasting daily sessions means resampling those events to a daily count first. Choose the frequency that matches the decision: daily for staffing, weekly for planning. The wrong frequency hides the very pattern you need.

Self-studyBuild the Lumen series on a datetime index (pandas)2 min read▶

Raw event logs are irregular. A resample turns them into a clean daily series:

★ Run it now (Python) - events to a daily seriesimport pandas as pd, numpy as np # Lumen web analytics: one row per session, with a timestamp rng = pd.date_range("2026-01-01", periods=20000, freq="17min") events = pd.DataFrame({"ts": rng, "session": 1}) # collapse to a clean DAILY series on a real datetime index s = (events.set_index("ts")["session"] .resample("D").sum() .asfreq("D")) # explicit daily frequency, no gaps print(s.head()) print("freq:", s.index.freq, "| missing days:", s.isna().sum())

That s - a value per day on a datetime index - is the input to every model from here.

Part 2 · covers FPP3 evaluation setup

Split by time, never at random 5 min live

The single most common forecasting bug is a random train/test split. Time only runs one way: you must train on the past and test on the future. Shuffle the rows and you leak tomorrow into today - your accuracy looks great and collapses in production.

LiveHoldout, and why a single split is only the start3 min▶

A simple time holdout is enough to begin: keep the last N periods as the test set and never look at them while modelling.

  • Holdout length = your real horizon. If you forecast 14 days ahead, hold out 14 days and score there.
  • One split is noisy. A single holdout can flatter or punish a model by luck. In b8 you will upgrade to rolling-origin backtesting - many splits marched forward through time.
  • Never tune on the test set. If you pick a model by peeking at test, you have leaked. Use a validation slice or backtest.
The horizon shapes everything A 7-day and a 90-day forecast are different problems with different best models and different uncertainty. Decide the horizon before you model, and evaluate at exactly that horizon.

Build the series and split it by time ★ 12 min · everyone builds

Turn Lumen's daily sessions into a clean series and carve off an honest holdout.

Load or build a daily series on a datetime index (use the sample below, or export your own non-confidential daily counts to CSV).

Resample to a fixed daily frequency and confirm there are no missing days.

Split by time: last 14 days as the test set, everything before as train. Print the two date ranges to prove test comes after train.

Plot it. Eyeball the trend and the weekly rhythm before you model anything.

★ Run it now (Python) - daily series + time splitimport pandas as pd, numpy as np # a Lumen-style daily sessions series: trend + weekly seasonality + noise idx = pd.date_range("2026-01-01", periods=112, freq="D") season = np.array([40,55,60,50,30,-120,-140]) # weekday shape i = np.arange(112) y = 1000 + 2.5*i + season[i % 7] + 14*np.sin(i*1.7) + 9*np.cos(i*0.6) s = pd.Series(np.round(y), index=idx, name="sessions") H = 14 # forecast horizon train, test = s.iloc[:-H], s.iloc[-H:] print("train:", train.index.min().date(), "->", train.index.max().date()) print("test :", test.index.min().date(), "->", test.index.max().date()) assert train.index.max() < test.index.min() # no leakage
Plot first, model second Half of forecasting is looking. Before any model, plot the series and its weekly and monthly averages. The trend, the seasonal period, and the outliers are usually visible to the eye - and they tell you which method on the ladder to reach for.

Ship the seasonal-naive baseline ★ 8 min · build your own

Before any clever model, build the baseline it has to beat. For a weekly-seasonal series the honest baseline is seasonal-naive: tomorrow equals the same weekday last week. It is two lines, and on seasonal data it is shockingly hard to beat - which is exactly why you measure against it.

Forecast each future day as the value from 7 days earlier (the last observed same weekday).

Score it on the holdout with MAPE (mean absolute percentage error).

Keep it - in b8 you will measure every model against this line, and in b3 you will watch Holt-Winters leave it behind.

★ Run it now (Python) - seasonal-naive baselinem = 7 # weekly seasonality history = train.copy() preds = [] for step in range(H): preds.append(history.iloc[-m + (step % m)]) # same weekday last cycle fc = pd.Series(preds, index=test.index) mape = (np.abs(test.values - fc.values) / test.values).mean() * 100 print(f"seasonal-naive MAPE: {mape:.1f}% (the line every model must beat)")
Real world

Never skip the baseline. The M-competitions - the field's biggest empirical bake-offs - show again and again that simple methods and their combinations are extremely hard to beat. If your ARIMA or neural net cannot beat seasonal-naive on a backtest (b8), it is not earning its complexity. The baseline is also your fallback when a fancy model breaks in production.

Three questions before you go 🎯 ◐ 90 seconds

1 · What are the building blocks of a time series?

A series is a sum of a slow trend, a repeating seasonality, a longer cycle, and irregular noise. Forecasting separates the persistent signal from the unforecastable noise.

2 · Why must a time series be split by time, not at random?

Time runs one way. Train on the past, test on the future. Shuffling lets the model peek at the future - the accuracy is a mirage.

3 · Why build the seasonal-naive baseline first?

On seasonal data, "same weekday last week" is surprisingly strong. If a complex model cannot beat it on a backtest (b8), it is not earning its complexity.
